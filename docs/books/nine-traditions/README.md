# The Nine Traditions — From Civilization Dawn to the Age of Machinery

*South of Tethys — editorial story architecture; **proposed, not canon***

## Scope

This collection proposes a **human-scale bridge** between the Harappan Civilization Dawn and the Age of Machinery and Exploration. Its nine narrative threads take **superficial thematic inspiration** from Bön, Sanamahi, Donyi-Polo, Sarna, Gondi Dharma, Niam Khasi, Songsarek, Toda religion and Kondh traditions. They are **not depictions of actual religious deities, rituals, or historical migrations**. Their inspired characters, names and institutions below are entirely fictional placeholders, not claims about living communities.

Existing canonical figures and settlements remain primary wherever their records already establish them. New characters must earn their place through trade, law, technology, ecological conflict and intergenerational transmission—not by retroactively founding real religions in the setting.

## Continuity guardrails

1. **Chronology:** Begin after the founding struggles of Lothal within Civilization Dawn; progress across intermediate generations toward the Age of Machinery, without assigning Gregorian dates or invented absolute year counts.
2. **Canon authority:** These are university-style speculative manuscripts, **not structured game canon**. Do not modify `database/`, character lineage, major events, or the core timeline until individual proposals are approved and reconciled.
3. **Superficial borrowing only:** Borrow motifs such as a sacred mountain, serpent, sun and moon, ancestral grove, guardian, buffalo, or stewardship of the land. Do not import worship practices, sacred narratives, divine identities or ethnic stereotypes wholesale.
4. **People are not language families:** Gond and Toda/Kondh associations differ from Khasi, Santal/Munda and Garo; do not flatten these living peoples into three fixed racial or phenotypic groups.
5. **No divine takeover:** Protagonists are mortal humans who may carry titles, legends or symbolic objects. Historical gods, founders and teachers are **not** inserted as historical rulers of Jambhudweepa.
6. **Technology has causes:** Trace progression through surplus, apprenticeships, records, waterways, metallurgy, printing, instrumentation, and eventually steam and telegraph networks; avoid sudden gifts of machines from a deity.
7. **Multiple viewpoints:** Each thread should expose both benefits and costs of state formation and industrialization, including dispossession, environmental damage and competing interpretations of archival evidence.
8. **Open end:** The Age of Machinery is the story's endpoint. Any Great Shattering or post-cataclysmic future remains a possible horizon, not an inevitable historical fact established here.

## The historical arc

### Book I — Civilization Dawn: Nine Witnesses
Lothal's city-building and trade networks create opportunities and pressures on the margins of the Saraswati system. Each witness has an ordinary responsibility—grain, grazing, stone, route, household, woodland, metal or tide. Their encounters with Harappan and Kia institutions create contracts and unresolved disputes rather than instant religious kingdoms.

### Book II — The Centuries of Exchange
The original witnesses die. Their descendants, rivals or adopted apprentices transmit imperfect versions of their knowledge. A grove covenant may become a forest statute; a sky ledger becomes an observatory's almanac; a forge compact becomes a mine lease. The plot is about how institutions preserve some memories and distort others.

### Book III — The Guilds and the Surveyors
Lothal's industrial guilds, the maritime networks of Vanga and the Narmada scholarly milieu begin consolidating older regional practices. An interregional dispute over forests, metal, pasture and inland passages brings the nine legacies into one archival record. Not all traditions consent to incorporation.

### Book IV — The Age of Machinery: The Ninth Register
A Narmada archivist working alongside engineers and community delegates compares nine contradictory registers while a proposed railway-and-canal expansion advances. Steam, telegraphy and survey law demonstrate the technical reach of the modern states; testimonies from peripheral settlements reveal their human cost. The conclusion is not that older customs vanish, but that their bearers decide what to translate, protect, reform or reject.

## Proposed crossover structure

- **Shared object:** a nine-part register of agreements, each copied, mistranslated and contested across generations; it is a narrative device, not an ancient sacred book.
- **Shared geographic axis:** Lothal and the Saraswati waterways → Narmada plateau archives → Vanga coast and the highland approaches. Exact placement awaits the canonical atlas.
- **Shared question:** Who owns the knowledge, water, roads and minerals on which an industrial civilization depends?
- **Returning institutions:** Harappan engineering and trade, Kia marsh custom, Tuli/Vanga maritime exchange and Narmada scholarship, only where supported by existing lore.
- **Character discipline:** The nine original witnesses are **not immortal**. Their legacy is carried by descendants, successors, material records and disputed memories.

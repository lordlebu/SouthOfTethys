# Singbonga, Marang Buru and Jaher Era: The Grove Testimonies
*The fourth register — three different witnesses from woodland and hill communities*

Accounts bearing the names **Singbonga**, **Marang Buru** and **Jaher Era** come from separate woodland and hill testimonies. They must not be folded into one ethnic community or single family. This fiction draws a superficial inspiration from sacred-grove traditions associated with different peoples; it is not a claim that the real deities were historical mortals.

## Civilization Dawn
Singbonga is remembered as a daylight boundary witness who insisted that grazing routes be walked openly before an oath could be sworn. Marang Buru, in a hill-side account, serves as a negotiator over stone and timber. Jaher Era is described as the custodian of a grove where families shelter during floods and bury their dead.

The three names enter a later legal roll when a caravan seeks timber, a quarry seeks basalt and newly settled families need a safe crossing. No contemporary source proves they met. The roll is a **later compilation of related obligations**, not a transcript of a council of three.

## Inherited dispute
A commercial scribe copies the grove perimeter as a timber concession, leaving out the clause protecting graves and spring water. Subsequent elders contest the record. A grove may remain a grove even when every household carrying the first witness's name has vanished.

## Age of Machinery
Industrial charcoal burners and rail surveyors invoke the old concession while residents cite a different copy. The Narmada Academic Republic archives both. Lothal workshops demand fuel; regional communities demand that the city recognize limits it did not create. The nineteenth-year docket of a machine-age ministry, whatever its number, cannot erase the older landscape.

# Tatara-Rabuga and Saljong: The Grain Ledger
*The seventh register — two human names in the eastern hillside testimony*

In an eastern agricultural account **Tatara-Rabuga** names an organizer of field allocations, while **Saljong** names a keeper of seed grain and harvest announcements. The reconstruction borrows only the broad motifs of creation and harvest associated with Garo/Songsarek traditions. It is not the history of that religion.

## Civilization Dawn
Several villages face the same poor harvest. Tatara-Rabuga argues that the best flood-safe ridge plots should not be monopolized by the settlement with the largest boats. Saljong carries measured seed between households and records whose grain is owed back after the next planting. A traveling Tuli merchant later transmits an abridged account to coastal traders.

## The passage of records
The ledgers change with crops, measures and languages. The old exchange was not necessarily a centralized taxation system; later bureaucrats may have read it that way because they could not imagine a store without a ruler.

## Age of Machinery
Rail-fed grain depots and merchant warehouses near the Vanga routes use standardized sacks and invoices. Farmers invoke Saljong's principle of retaining enough grain for another sowing, while exporters cite the same name as the father of predictable surplus. Neither side has a monopoly on his memory. The unresolved issue is **food security versus distant commercial demand**.

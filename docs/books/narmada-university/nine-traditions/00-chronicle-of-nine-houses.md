# The Nine Houses: From Civilization Dawn to the Mechanical Registers
*An inquiry into nineteen remembered people and the institutions that outlived them*

## The University's premise
The nine names in this collection are modern cataloguing conveniences for bodies of testimony whose own histories were not uniform. Later religious epithets have attached to nineteen persons: a mountain teacher, hearth custodians, sky observers, grove negotiators, metallurgists, assembly witnesses, coastal agriculturists, pastoral founders and earth guardians. In this **South of Tethys fictional reconstruction**, these named figures are investigated as **human beings**, neither imported gods nor verified immortals. The real traditions of Bön, Sanamahi, Donyi-Polo, Sarna, Gondi Dharma, Niam Khasi, Songsarek, Toda and Kondh supply only superficial motifs, not purported biographies.

The University cannot prove that every account concerns one historical individual rather than an office, epithet, or several people conflated over generations. Its task is to set out possible human histories and test what reached later cities. These volumes are new, speculative literature; they do not amend the game database or established canon.

## I. The margin of the cities
In Civilization Dawn the Harappan city-world expanded along the Saraswati without inventing all the peoples beyond its walls. Lothal's Mask Family, Kia marsh customs, Vedda frontiers, Naga courts, Tuli seafarers and other existing communities retained histories of their own. The nineteen appear **alongside**, not before the earliest migrations and not as founders of established cultures.

The first testimonies concern practical disputes: shelter on a winter pass, an unextinguished household fire, equitable allocation of river light and tide, burial groves, iron ore, spoken judgments, seed grain, buffalo pasture and mountain springs. From such work, later households could remember a person's name as a protective title.

## II. How a person becomes a legend
A remembered administrator may become a spirit in a song. A skilled teacher may become a timeless founder. A hill boundary stone may gain a patron's face. That change is an episode of **historical remembrance**, not proof that the person was divine, and not a description of how an actual living religion arose. The University preserves discrepancies instead of flattening them.

## III. The receiving world
The Harappan-Lothal Industrial Union did not inherit every hearth or forge. The Narmada Academic Republic did not discover or own every stone record. The Vanga Maritime Federation, the Shwetavahana Riverine League, the Hyrkanian and Tushara Rail-Khanates and other powers likewise encountered competing regional institutions. The record must distinguish adoption by contract, instruction, coercion, trade and independent parallel invention.

## IV. The nineteenth century that cannot be dated here
The Age of Machinery should be described by its own era-name rather than imported Gregorian dates. Its guild workshops, coal corridors, canals, printing rooms, legal registries and telegraph routes carry old arguments into new devices. What was a dispute over a forest edge may become a dispute over industrial charcoal; what was an obligation to keep a spring clear may become a municipal water concession.

## V. The Nine Registers
Each following account identifies a cluster of names, a possible Civilization Dawn incident, a chain of transmission across generations, and an Age of Machinery counterpart. **No original protagonist physically survives the intervening centuries.** A later user of the name may be a namesake, a title-bearer, an admirer or a complete stranger.

## Open questions
For every witness: what is the earliest local attestation; who recorded the testimony; was the name personal, inherited or titular; where was the first contact with existing settlements; which intermediate records, if any, bridge the centuries; and what independent witnesses disagree? None of these questions may be answered solely by resemblance to present-day religious vocabulary.

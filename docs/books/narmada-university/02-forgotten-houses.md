# The Forgotten Houses: Claims to an Asura Past

*Studies in Ancient Testimony, Volume II*  
*Narmada University — Collegium of Historical Inquiry*

## Preface
A house may survive as a name long after its court has vanished. Among the Asura claimants, the problem is sharpened by a world of interrupted passage: one community remembers a gate, another a treaty, and a third a set of disciplines no longer understood.

## I. The Names of Houses
The old lists contain the names of great Asura figures and their supposed followers. They do not establish an uninterrupted succession to any later settlement. Court historians have sometimes presented parallel legends as a single chain of command; such reconstructions remain contested.

## II. The Archive of Offices
Some institutions are more durable than bloodlines. The office of Keeper, the right to breed beasts for war, and the preservation of rites can pass between strangers. An inherited office may generate a powerful claim to ancient authority while leaving the bearer unrelated to its founder.

## III. The Question of the Gate
A passage described in one epoch cannot be assumed to occupy the same coordinates in another. The Dwarka Gate and the Ancient Courts appear in records of differing specificity. The University therefore declines to manufacture a single fixed itinerary for the ancient houses.

## IV. Appropriation and Legitimation
An ambitious court may adopt an Elder's emblem to justify conquest. An initiate may repeat a lost formula without understanding its origin. Such acts are historically important even if the asserted ancestry is false.

## V. What Remains
The houses are best approached as contested repositories of memory: fragments of law, instruments of ritual, schools of craft and disputed sovereign names. The absence of a family tree does not render their influence imaginary.

## Closing Judgment
An archive can establish that a claim was made. It cannot, without independent evidence, establish that the claim was true.

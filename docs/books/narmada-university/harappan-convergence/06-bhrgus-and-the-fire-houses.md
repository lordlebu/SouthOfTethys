# The Bhṛgus: Fire Houses across the Divided Frontier
*Workshop, oath and divided allegiance*

## A name carried by more than one hearth
The Bhṛgus appear in the old songs as a lineage associated with fire and ritual expertise; the war cycle also names them among Sudās's opponents. These facts must be kept together. A learned house may carry authority in ritual without being politically loyal to whichever king commissions its members.

Within the University's comparative reconstruction, Bhṛgu may denote an ancestral name, a set of teaching houses and political followers at different times. No single unbroken pedigree is recoverable from the current archive.

## Work of the custodians
The fire houses are best understood through the practical duties that might keep a tradition alive: maintaining flame safely in travel, selecting suitable fuel and hearth material, witnessing the sharing of food, certifying exchanged goods and remembering the obligations sworn before a gathering.

The University's hypothesis is that craftsmen and judges learned some of these procedures from Bhṛgu custodians during the expansion of trade. This does not give the Bhṛgus ownership of every later forge, furnace or sacred hearth. Existing Harappan stone-engineers, Kia artisans and local fire traditions retain their independent histories.

## The divided war
In the conflict remembered under Sudās's name, a Bhṛgu contingent appears on the opposing side. The record does not prove that every member of the lineage fought for that coalition. Some might have refused service, remained with their households or attended wounded on both sides. Without separate witnesses, these remain possibilities.

Later victorious patrons may have found it convenient to erase Bhṛgu participation among their opponents; the University will not repair that omission by making the whole lineage Sudās's servants.

## Paths toward the cities
Fire knowledge is portable. An apprentice could carry a technique across a frontier when a royal title could not travel. Legal and ritual procedures could pass into a mixed workshop without displacing its older owner. A potter, stoneworker, metallurgist and priest could collaborate without being members of one tribe.

For Civilization Dawn, the University proposes studying furnace precincts, apprenticeship oaths, household fires, trade guarantees and workshop arbitration. The first identifiable Bhṛgu-owned Harappan workshop remains undiscovered.

## Later inheritance
The Age of Machinery's Harappan-Lothal Industrial Union maintains foundries, clockwork factories, copper solar-coil workshops and patent registers. Those institutions have established Harappan, Mask Family and Kia inheritances. A Bhṛgu contribution, if demonstrated, would be one thread among several: perhaps the wording of an apprentice oath or an older technique of furnace custody.

Similarity alone does not establish descent. A machine-age master of iron may respect a Bhṛgu maxim without being a Bhṛgu by lineage.

## University tests
A comparison of fire vocabulary between the Bhṛgu song cycle and civic workshop contracts; evidence for craft migration after the coalition war; a dated chain of texts or material practices; and testimony from craftspeople who never entered Harappan jurisdiction.

The chronicle's governing question is not whether the Bhṛgus *became* Harappa, but **which parts of their scattered knowledge were accepted, altered or refused by older Harappan institutions**.

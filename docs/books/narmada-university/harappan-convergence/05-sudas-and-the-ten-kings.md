# Sudās and the War of the Ten Kings
*The Paruṣṇī cycle and the difficulty of a victor's account*

## A royal inheritance
Sudās appears as a ruler of the Bharata and Tṛtsu political world, praised in the songs of Vasiṣṭha. Divodāsa is called his father in one of the central surviving verses. This is a useful dynastic anchor, not authority to populate every unrecorded generation.

Several forms of Sudās's name and titles survive. Whether all refer to one reign in every local record remains a question for paleographers.

## Before the war
The conflict presupposed a countryside whose inhabitants already had claims: routes maintained by households, seasonal camps, livestock access, cultivated ground and agreements of hospitality. The rise of Sudās did not create these things. His claim was that he could protect, reorder or extend them.

Viśvāmitra's river-passage tradition remembers Sudās in another setting. The Vasiṣṭha tradition preserves the great conflict. What happened between these bodies of song is imperfectly known; a precise change of royal priest or an elaborate personal feud should not be treated as a witnessed sequence.

## The coalition
The familiar name *War of the Ten Kings* conceals a difficult record. The victorious song names more persons and groupings than a simple list of ten kings. Among the figures and peoples appearing in the cycle are the Bhṛgus, Druhyus, Anus, Pūrus, Pakthas, Bhalānas, Alinas, Śivas, Viṣāṇins and others. Not all have the same status as names: some could be peoples, some ruling factions, others fighting bands or titles.

The Bhṛgu households may have supported an opposing coalition while other Bhṛgu singers maintained separate obligations. A ritual lineage need not have cast a single vote.

The names of the opponents are not to be converted into a modern map of ten neatly bordered kingdoms.

## The battle and the river
The account returns repeatedly to water, forced passage, threatened troops and loss. Sudās's side remembered victory, many enemies and the protection of Indr. The celestial Indr existed beyond the ambitions of either faction; the surviving hymn is an appeal and proclamation from one side, not the testimony of every soldier. The University's historical duty is to record the proclamation as such.

The precise battlefield location on Jambhudweepa is **not established**. The Paruṣṇī and other river-names in comparative song are evidence for an inherited narrative, not permission to rename a canon river.

## The aftermath: not one fate for the defeated
Military defeat could dissolve a royal alliance without extinguishing the peoples that fought under it. Some families may have remained farmers, some offered service to rival courts, and some sought passage to coastal markets. Displaced scribes, ritual specialists and metalworkers might have sold their knowledge in Harappan towns.

These are **alternative pathways for investigation**, not a claim that all of Anu, Druhyu, Pūru and Bhṛgu became Harappan subjects immediately after the war.

## Memory in the Harappan city-world
One archive could call Sudās a protector of trade; another could remember a blockade or confiscation in his name. The city-state period had room for competing commemorations. If a Lothal guild later adopted a Bharata oath or a Bhṛgu hearth practice, that transfer could be reciprocal and generations removed from the original fighting.

No established Lothal event is displaced by this war. The Stone Pact, the founding of Lothal, the Black Lotus Siege and the Tendua Crisis retain their separate documented sequence.

## Contested questions
- Can the University recover testimony from outside Sudās's victorious tradition?
- Which names designate self-conscious peoples, and which are accusations or temporary alliances?
- Did any defeated household enter a known Harappan ward?
- Were river crossings the war's cause, its setting, or both?
- Which rites were political propaganda, and which belonged to everyday riverine practice?

**Verdict:** The war is a powerful candidate for explaining the rearrangement of frontier alliances. A proven direct chain from the battle to Harappan unification has not yet been found.

# Nārada and the Travelling Witnesses
*An inquiry into singers who crossed political borders*

## A voice associated with the Kāṇvas
The name Nārada survives in hymn attributions associated with the Kāṇva tradition, and is paired with Parvata in another series of assignments. A traditional heading may name a composer, teaching lineage or recognized custodian of a poem. It does not supply a full itinerary or biography.

The University has no confirmed record that Nārada personally witnessed the war of Sudās, met Kashyapa of the Vedda or visited the Naga-Raja's court. The chronological distances between those histories have not been resolved.

## The witness's vocation
In the University's adopted late migration-period portrait, Nārada represents a type of travelling specialist whose power lay in remembering words accurately between courts. A witness could recite an oath, challenge a false genealogy, retain the terms of a cattle exchange, or carry a complaint across a frontier.

Such a specialist depended upon hospitality and could become dangerous to a ruler who preferred forgetfulness. The stories of a messenger permitted entry to rival camps may have grown from the political value of remembered speech.

## Courts and disputed testimony
The northern confederations, river merchants and coastal rulers did not necessarily employ the same calendars or witnesses. A Kāṇva singer's testimony might be respected in one assembly and rejected in another. It could be translated by Vedda interpreters or tested against a Naga court record.

The University finds the competing testimonies more valuable than a legend in which Nārada could travel everywhere without contradiction.

## Into the Harappan record
A city needs records that can be trusted when the original witnesses have died. This provides a plausible path from itinerant recitation to tablet, seal, guild roll and municipal oath. It is not yet proof that a named Harappan scribal school descends directly from Nārada.

A working study should compare repetition formulae, methods of preserving the names of witnesses, and sanctions for dishonest testimony.

## The late age
Narmada University's own travelling surveyors, excavators and oral historians inherit an analogous problem: a song heard far from its home must not be reported as a document found at its place of origin. The comparison makes Nārada's case a useful lesson for present-day scholarship.

## The separated names
Kāṇva attributions and the Vedda memory of Kashyapa are preserved in separate registers until a dated chain connects them. Later narrative honours attached to Nārada may be treasured as literature, but they are not evidence that one historical traveller lived across multiple epochs.

The University places Nārada in the late Era of Human Migrations (epoch_migrations), in the broader scholarly milieu of Divodāsa, Sudās, Viśvāmitra and Vasiṣṭha. His specific place, generation and personal encounters remain unconfirmed. This is an adopted manuscript placement, not a claim of a revised database record.

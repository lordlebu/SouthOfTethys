# Divodāsa and the Frontier Houses
*Fragments concerning a king before Sudās*

## The earlier name
Divodāsa stands in the praise tradition as a ruler remembered before Sudās. The University treats the name as an earlier royal figure, but avoids manufacturing an uninterrupted succession of named monarchs between the two. One verse directly calls Divodāsa the father of Sudās; other kinship and patronymic formulae require comparison before a royal genealogy can be written.

The oldest narratives of the frontier are crowded with people whose settlement did not depend on a king's permission. A claim that Divodāsa won a ford does not prove that he founded the village beside it. A claim that a household served him does not prove that its later descendants served Sudās.

## The frontier economy
A king of the passage-country exercised authority less by owning every field than by securing practical obligations: maintaining safe crossings, arbitrating cattle claims, escorting travelling craftworkers, guaranteeing seasonal water, and returning stolen goods or accepting compensation. These are plausible powers in the transition between migration and urban exchange; the surviving local archives have not yet confirmed the exact laws used by Divodāsa.

In the University's reconstruction, his court is therefore a mobile political centre rather than the owner of a mapped empire. Some of his allies may have been Vedda guides, Naga traders, older inhabitants, or younger migrant houses. Their identities remain open.

## The songs and their silences
Divodāsa is remembered through acts of favour, conflict and prayer, often in language invoking Indr. Indr's celestial existence belongs to established history, but a prayer naming him is not evidence that every victory was witnessed by independent observers. The university distinguishes an appeal, an attributed favour and a securely documented event.

We possess no reliable inventory of Divodāsa's fortresses, no confirmed burial, and no trustworthy map of his court's marches. His title and fame survive more securely than his borders.

## Inheritance and rupture
Sudās may have inherited obligations as well as enemies. Houses expecting the continuation of older contracts could react violently when the next ruler changed his escorts, ritual authorities or rights of passage. That is one possible political prehistory of the coalition war.

It is equally possible that the war arose from unrelated local causes. The University will not force the king's death, a particular marriage or a named betrayal into the chronology without a witness.

## Later Harappan intersections
The most promising material is not a direct claim that Divodāsa founded a city. It is the history of frontier institutions that a city might have adopted: recognized caravan guarantors, toll settlements, river ferries, travelling adjudicators and compensatory law. Some of these may have entered the Lothal world by the time its markets were established. Their lineage is presently a working reconstruction.

**Questions for excavation:** Are there two independent court lists naming Divodāsa? Is any passage-house attested by both a frontier oath and a city ledger? Which rituals were adopted without transfer of political sovereignty?
